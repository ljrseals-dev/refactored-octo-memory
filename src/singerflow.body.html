SingerFlow

Three practice tools for singers: a live formant tuner, a consonant coach called Blow! Singer, and a gospel run builder. Style ideas come from published analysis of Mahalia Jackson's singing. Nothing here imitates or synthesizes her voice.

Each faint line is a harmonic of your sung note. Singers often tune F1 onto a harmonic so a vowel rings louder with less effort. Formant estimates get less reliable as pitch rises, because the harmonics become too sparse to outline the vocal tract. In tests on synthetic vowels the readings were close up to about 260 Hz (middle C) and tended to lock onto a harmonic from about 330 Hz (E4) upward, so treat higher notes as rough.

Lyric line

Bars show one beat per word: the colored start is the consonant before the beat, the purple middle is the vowel, the blue end is the release. Lead times are starting estimates to tune by ear. Spelling-based parsing is approximate: silent letters, soft c and unusual words can be misread.

Held note, then a run

The pitch guide is a plain synthesized tone for hearing the contour. It is not a voice model.

Settings

Method: learn the run on a consonant, keep it clean at 80 BPM, add 5 BPM per clean pass, then move to a narrow vowel and finally the lyric vowel.

What this is built on

Published analysis of Mahalia Jackson's recordings (HfM Weimar pop voices study) describes a vibrato near 6 to 7 Hz, a glide into long notes, runs of about 4 to 7 notes at the end of long notes, short phrases with breaths inside them, and almost entirely pentatonic note choice. Consonant and run practice follows common voice-teaching guidance. Formant tuning follows the harmonic-proximity idea in the Finding Resonance tool: distance from a formant to the nearest harmonic in cents.

Microphone audio is analysed in your browser and is never uploaded. Reference vowel values are approximate speech averages and shift in singing. This is a practice aid, not a substitute for a teacher.
