A Note on Method

This is not a biography. It is a scoring of possibilities.

The London Fog style, as deployed here, refuses the clean verdict. It insists that the historical record—particularly for a Black woman born into poverty in the Ninth Ward of New Orleans in 1911—is never fully transparent. The fog is not evasion. It is fidelity to the conditions of the archive: fragmentary, contradictory, redacted by respectability and by the violence of segregation.

The Brute Genius framework, described in Part III, provides the analytical spine. It is a tool for exhaustive combinatorial analysis: given a discrete set of documented influences, practices, and biographical fragments, it enumerates every possible narrative sequence and ranks them by coherence with the full dataset. It does not prove. It ranks.

The book proceeds in four movements. Part I establishes the occult ecology of New Orleans. Part II traces the formation of Mahalia Jackson as a medium—a term used here in its neutral, technical sense, denoting one who transmits. Part III applies the Brute Genius tool to the question of her conversion. Part IV translates the tools into a realtime architecture, closing the loop between historiography and executable code.

The prose is atmospheric. The analysis is exhaustive. The verdict is withheld—not because a verdict is impossible, but because the highest-ranked hypothesis is not a verdict at all. It is a fog.

PROLOGUE: THE SHOTGUN SHACK AND THE VEIL

0.1 The Geography of the Veil

Water Street, New Orleans, 1911. The levee to the east. The railroad tracks to the west. Between them, a strip of unpainted shotgun shacks, row after row, their thin walls breathing the river air, their floors sweating in the summer heat. Halie Jackson was born here—or hereabouts; the records are imprecise—the third child of Charity and John Jackson.

The geography matters. The levee is the boundary between the city and the river, between the built world and the natural force that could swallow it. The tracks are the boundary between the neighborhood and the industrial world, between the local and the transient. The shotgun shack is the boundary between the family and the street, between the domestic and the public. And the church—there were two, one on each side—is the boundary between the sacred and the secular.

Halie grew up on boundaries. She grew up in a space that was neither fully inside nor fully outside. W. E. B. Du Bois named this condition "double-consciousness"—the sense of always looking at oneself through the eyes of another. But Du Bois was writing about race. The double-consciousness of Halie Jackson was also sonic. It was spiritual. It was the awareness of living between two systems of meaning.

0.2 Two Sounds, One Throat

Mount Moriah Baptist Church stood on one side of her childhood. A Sanctified church—storefront, unrecorded in denominational histories, known only through memoir and memory—stood on the other.

The Baptist church was orderly. The hymn lined out by the clerk. The congregation seated, then standing, then seated again. The piano, or the organ, or the human voice alone, carrying the tune. The music was a vessel for the Word. It was text set to melody, doctrine made audible.

The Sanctified church was otherwise. The drum—there was almost always a drum—led the worship. The congregation moved. The body swayed, jerked, fell. The music was a vessel for the Spirit. It was not the Word but the presence, not doctrine but transport.

Halie heard both. She heard the hymn and the drum. She heard the lined-out meter and the improvised shout. She heard the orderly and the ecstatic. And she took both into her throat.

Later, she would say that she "loved things at Mount Moriah, but loved other things, too." This is not the statement of a woman who chose one tradition over another. It is the statement of a woman who carried both.

0.3 The Occult Question

Standard biographies of Mahalia Jackson flatten her. They present her as a devout Christian girl who sang gospel with a distinctive style, without asking where that style came from. They present her conversion as a clean break from the secular (she loved the blues as a child) to the sacred (she sang only gospel as an adult). They present her as a saint.

But the record does not support this flattening. The record shows a child who handled snakes in the ditches around Audubon Park, who listened to Bessie Smith records at her cousin's house, who watched the dice-shakers and card-players on Magazine Street, who dreamed of statues and saw visions, who described her mature performance as a form of self-loss—"I am transformed from Mahalia Jackson into something divine."

This is not the language of Baptist orthodoxy. This is the language of trance. This is the language of a tradition that has been systematically erased from the respectable record but that survived in the bodies and voices of Black women.

The central provocation of this book is that Mahalia Jackson's conversion was not a rupture. It was a resolution. She did not abandon the occult ecology of her childhood. She found a Baptist frame large enough to hold it.

PART I: THE OCCULT ECOLOGY OF NEW ORLEANS

---

Chapter 1: The Spiritual Marketplace of the Ninth Ward

1.1 Hoodoo, Conjure, and the Sanctified Church

New Orleans in the early twentieth century was a spiritual marketplace. Hoodoo and conjure—African-derived traditions of rootwork, herbalism, and spiritual intervention—had been present since the colonial period. The city's Catholic churches coexisted with the voodoo queens, the gris-gris women, the readers and the healers. Black New Orleans, no less than white New Orleans, navigated a plural universe of spiritual technologies.

The Sanctified church was one response to this plurality. It emerged from the Holiness movement of the late nineteenth century, but in Black hands it took on a distinctly African character. The Sanctified church embraced what the mainstream Black churches had learned to fear: the drum, the dance, the trance. It did not separate the Spirit from the body. It understood the body as the Spirit's instrument.

This was a scandal to the Baptists. They called the Sanctified worship "unseemly." They distrusted the trances, the fits, the shouting. But they could not deny the power.

1.2 The Storefront Next Door

The Sanctified church next to the Jackson home on Water Street does not appear in the standard histories. It is known only through Mahalia's own recollections and through the broader pattern of the Sanctified presence in Black neighborhoods. But what we know is enough to reconstruct its character.

It was a storefront. It was small. It was loud. The drum was there—whether a real drum or a makeshift one—and the music was driven by it. The worship was embodied. There were trances, shouts, falls. And there was a reputation that extended beyond the church's walls.

Baptists regarded this church with a complex mixture of fascination and fear. It was beautiful—the music was undeniably powerful—but it was dangerous. The Spirit that moved in the Sanctified church was not the orderly Spirit of Baptist doctrine. It was the unruly Spirit of the shout, the Spirit that could not be contained by doctrine or discipline.

1.3 Halie as an Observer

Halie Jackson was a child who watched. She watched the Sanctified church through its open door. She watched the bodies moving, the mouths open, the eyes closed. She heard the drum and the cry. She was not inside, but she was not outside either. She was at the threshold.

She carried this threshold moment into her later life. Her statement that she "loved things at Mount Moriah, but loved other things, too" is an act of fidelity to both traditions. She refused to choose. She refused to disavow what she had seen and heard at the Sanctified church. The drums got in her bones.

---

Chapter 2: Dreams, Visions, and the Death of Charity Jackson

2.1 The Mother's Death

Charity Jackson died when Halie was five years old. This event is the central trauma of her childhood, but the biographies tend to narrate it as a simple tragedy—a mother's death, a daughter's grief—without asking what it meant in the spiritual ecology of the community.

Death in Black New Orleans was not simply a biological event. It was a spiritual passage. The dying could have visions. The living could dream of the dead. The boundary between worlds was thin.

Charity's death, then, was not just the loss of a mother. It was the opening of a door between worlds. Halie was five years old, and she was looking through that door.

2.2 The Dream of the Statue

Mahalia later recalled a dream involving a religious statue—the details are vague, the statue probably Catholic, probably the Virgin or a saint—that occurred shortly before her own hospitalization for a leg infection. In the dream, the statue was alive. It spoke to her.

This dream is typically narrated as a sign of her precocious piety. But read through the lens of New Orleans dream-work traditions, it is something else. It is a form of spiritual contact. The statue is not a symbol; it is an agent. The dream is not a sign of faith; it is a mode of access.

2.3 Aunt Duke and the Law of the Switch

Aunt Duke, the woman who raised Halie after her mother's death, was a strict Baptist. She imposed a regime of corporal punishment and public apology. When Halie played too much, she was whipped. When she disobeyed, she was forced to stand before the congregation and confess her sin.

This regime was not simply discipline. It was exorcism. Aunt Duke understood that the child was at risk of the "disease" of play—the uncleanliness of the unruly body—and she used every tool at her disposal to drive it out. The switch was the instrument. The public apology was the rite.

But the cure is also the diagnosis. Aunt Duke's severity testifies to the power of what she was trying to suppress. She was not whipping a small child for the fun of it. She was whipping a small child who carried something dangerous.

---

Chapter 3: The Secular Sacred—Blues, Gamblers, and the Unruly Body

3.1 Magazine Street as a Rival Liturgy

On payday, the streets of Black New Orleans became a theatre of the secular. The dice-shakers and card-players, the hustlers and the blues singers, the dancers and the lovers—all of them filled the sidewalks and the corners, offering a rival liturgy of the body.

Halie watched. She was not allowed to participate, but she watched. The secular sacred was not the absence of spirit. It was a different spirit, a spirit of the flesh, a spirit of the present moment. The gambler was in his own kind of trance. The blues singer was in her own kind of transport.

3.2 Bessie Smith and Cousin Fred's Records

Cousin Fred had records. Bessie Smith, Ma Rainey, the classic blues singers of the 1920s. Halie listened.

The blues was an alternative sonic technology of spirit. It did not lead to the Baptist God. It led to the body, to the heart, to the complicated emotions of everyday life. Bessie Smith sang about love and loss, about sex and betrayal, about the wages of being Black and a woman in America. This was not gospel. But it was not simply secular either. It was a form of truth-telling that the church could not contain.

3.3 The Tomboy and the Snake

Halie was a tomboy. She caught crawfish in the ditches, ran barefoot, handled snakes.

The snake is a polysemous symbol. In the Baptist tradition, it is the tempter, the devil, the evil one. In the hoodoo tradition, it is wisdom, power, healing. The snake that Halie handled was not simply a snake. It was a participant in a spiritual economy that the church was trying to suppress.

The child who handles snakes is a child who is not afraid of the forbidden. She is a child who has already learned that the forbidden is often the place where power lives.

PART II: THE UPBRINGING—FORMATION OF A MEDIUM

---

Chapter 4: The Plymouth Rock Debut

4.1 Age Four

At age four, Halie Jackson appeared for the first time in the children's choir of Plymouth Rock Baptist Church. This is the debut. It is the moment when the child becomes a voice.

The fact that she was four years old is significant. She was not a trained singer. She was a child. The voice that came out of her was not the product of technique. It was the product of some deeper source.

4.2 The Voice as a Vehicle

The congregation interpreted the child's contralto—the low, rich voice, unusual for a child—as evidence of anointing. This language of anointing is a way of making sense of a voice that exceeds its apparent source. The voice is not simply the voice of the child; it is the voice of the Spirit speaking through the child.

This is possession-language. It is trance-language. Even in the Baptist context, the child's voice was understood as a vehicle for something greater.

4.3 The Junior Choir at Mount Moriah

Halie rose within the Baptist structure. She joined the junior choir at Mount Moriah, the church where her Aunt Duke was a member. She began to develop a performing self—a self that exceeded the liturgical script.

The Baptist church did not typically welcome the kind of excess that would later define Halie's style. But even in the junior choir, she began to test the boundaries. She began to find the space within the hymn where the Spirit could move.

---

Chapter 5: The Sanctified Swing

5.1 Drums Over Piano

The technical difference between Baptist and Sanctified music is the difference between piano and drum. The Baptist hymn is driven by melody and text. The Sanctified shout is driven by rhythm and percussion. The drum is not an accompaniment; it is the engine.

Halie absorbed this engine. Even when she sang Baptist hymns, the drum was in her body. Her phrasing, her timing, her use of syncopation—all of this came from the Sanctified rhythm. She did not abandon the drum when she entered the Baptist church. She internalized it.

5.2 "I Could Stay in Church All Day"

Mahalia described the church as the "right boat." It was the vessel that could carry her where she needed to go. She could stay in church all day, not because she was pious, but because the church was the place where the rhythm was alive, where the body could move, where the Spirit could be heard.

The church was a container for the Sanctified spirit that might otherwise have been read as unruly. It was a frame large enough to hold the drum.

5.3 The Jerks and Steps

Mahalia's later performance style was described as "influenced by the Southern sanctified style of keeping time with the body." She did not stand still. She did not suppress the physical. She swayed, she stepped, she moved. The body was an instrument of spiritual transmission.

This is the "jerk"—the sudden, involuntary movement that is a hallmark of Sanctified trance. It is a movement that comes from somewhere else, from a place that the conscious mind does not control. It is the body's testimony that something is present.

---

Chapter 6: The Chicago Rupture and the Baptist Reinvention

6.1 The Move at Fifteen

In 1927, at the age of fifteen, Halie Jackson moved to Chicago. This is the rupture. She left the New Orleans spiritual ecology and entered a new environment.

The Northern Baptist churches were different from the Southern ones. They were more formal. They were more respectable. They were more suspicious of the Sanctified excess. But they were also hungry for the old sound.

6.2 Greater Salem and the Johnson Brothers

Halie joined Greater Salem Baptist Church and began singing with the Johnson Brothers. This was her entry into the Northern gospel circuit. This was the professionalization of her gift.

The Johnson Brothers were also migrants from the South. They understood the Sanctified rhythm. They understood the drum. They gave Halie a space to develop her style within a Baptist frame.

6.3 The Conservative Backlash

But the Northern Baptist establishment was not unified. There were those who were "enraged" by Halie's performing style. They accused her of "worldliness." They said her music was not sacred.

This charge of worldliness is a coded charge of occult contamination. What they were objecting to was not simply the syncopation or the movement. They were objecting to the presence of the body, the presence of the drum, the presence of the Spirit in its unruly form. They were objecting to the Sanctified influence.

6.4 The Conversion Narrative

The standard biography narrates Halie's Chicago conversion as a clean break. She moved from the secular to the sacred. She abandoned the blues—she had loved Bessie Smith—and she gave herself entirely to gospel.

But the record does not support this clean break. The blues remained in her voice. The Sanctified rhythm remained in her body. Her conversion was not a rejection of her past; it was a synthesis.

---

Chapter 7: The Dream of the Divine

7.1 "I Am Transformed"

In 1963, Mahalia Jackson described her experience of singing as a mystical experience. "I don't seem to be myself," she said. "I am transformed from Mahalia Jackson into something divine."

This is a remarkable statement. It is the language of self-loss. It is the language of trance. She is not saying that she is singing well. She is saying that she is no longer herself.

7.2 The Medium and the Message

This self-loss is a theological problem for orthodox Christianity. The orthodox tradition insists on the integrity of the self, even in prayer and worship. The self is not erased; it is sanctified.

But Mahalia is not describing sanctification. She is describing dissolution. She is describing the moment when the "I" disappears and the Spirit speaks. This is Spiritualist trance, not Baptist prayer.

7.3 The Occult Remainder

What survives of the New Orleans spiritual ecology in her mature performance? The bodily swing. The percussive phrasing. The dream-logic of her improvisations. The refusal to separate the sacred from the secular. The language of transformation.

The occult remainder is the something that cannot be named by the Baptist frame. It is the drum that is still beating. It is the snake in the ditch. It is the vision of the statue. It is the self that is lost in the song.

PART III: THE CONVERSION—A BRUTE GENIUS ANALYSIS

---

Chapter 8: What Is Brute Genius?

8.1 Exhaustive Combinatorial Search

Brute Genius is a tool. It is designed for a specific purpose: given a finite search space, to find the optimal combination of variables. It does this by enumerating the full Cartesian product—every possible combination—and scoring each combination against a named objective.

The approach is called "brute" because it does not use heuristics or pruning. It does not try to be clever. It tries every possibility and evaluates the results.

8.2 Why This Matters for Biography

The standard conversion narrative is cherry-picked. It selects a few key events—the move to Chicago, the meeting with Thomas Dorsey, the decision to sing only gospel—and arranges them into a coherent story.

But the problem with cherry-picking is that it can produce any story you want. If you select different fragments, you can tell a different story. Brute Genius forces us to consider every documented fragment, every lyrical variant, every biographical moment. It forces us to confront the full dataset.

8.3 The Objective Function

For this book, the objective is "coherence with the documented spiritual ecology of Black New Orleans." This is not a mystical criterion. It is an empirical one. We have a documented record of what that ecology looked like—the hoodoo practices, the Sanctified worship, the dream-work, the snake-handling. We can measure how well any given narrative sequence fits that record.

The tool does not prove occult influence. It ranks hypotheses by their explanatory power.

---

Chapter 9: Encoding the Dataset

9.1 Sources

The dataset is drawn from interviews, song lyrics, biographical accounts, and the scholarly literature on New Orleans conjure and Sanctified worship.

Each source is coded for: spiritual practice (Baptist, Sanctified, hoodoo, blues secularism), bodily modality (stillness vs. movement), sonic signature (hymn vs. drum), and conversion timing (before, during, or after the Chicago move).

9.2 Variables

The variables are not independent. They are correlated in the documented record. The Sanctified church is correlated with drum and movement. The Baptist church is correlated with hymn and stillness. The blues is correlated with movement and secularism. Hoodoo is correlated with dream and ritual.

The tool must account for these correlations. It must not produce combinations that are disconnected from the historical record.

9.3 The Combinatorial Explosion

Even a modest dataset generates millions of possible sequences. If you have ten variables with three possible values each, you have 3^10 = 59,049 possibilities. If you have twenty variables, you have 3^20 = 3.4 billion possibilities.

Brute Genius does not prune. It evaluates all of them. This is computationally intensive, but it is necessary for the integrity of the analysis. The point is not to find a single answer. The point is to map the space of possible answers.

---

Chapter 10: The Ranked Hypotheses

10.1 Hypothesis A: Clean Baptist Conversion

Hypothesis A is the standard biography. Halie Jackson, raised in a Sanctified-tinged environment, moved to Chicago, experienced a conversion, and became a Baptist gospel singer who rejected the secular and the Sanctified alike.

The rank is low. The hypothesis is incoherent with the documented persistence of Sanctified style in her mature work. She did not reject the drum. She internalized it.

10.2 Hypothesis B: Sanctified Synthesis

Hypothesis B is the synthesis hypothesis. Halie Jackson's conversion was the integration of Baptist discipline and Sanctified release. She did not choose one over the other. She found a frame—gospel music—that could hold both.

The rank is high. The hypothesis is coherent with the full dataset. It explains why she could sing Baptist hymns with a Sanctified swing. It explains why she described her performances as transformation. It explains why she never abandoned the blues entirely—the blues was in her voice, even if she did not sing it.

10.3 Hypothesis C: Occult Continuity

Hypothesis C is the occult continuity hypothesis. Halie Jackson's conversion was not a conversion at all. It was a public renaming of a spiritual practice that was continuous with the occult ecology of her childhood. The dream-work, the snake-handling, the trance-language—all of this suggests a spiritualist substrate that was never erased, only renamed.

The rank is moderate. The hypothesis is coherent with the dream evidence and the trance language, but it is not consistent with the Baptist discipline that Halie also adopted. She was not simply a spiritualist who called herself a Baptist. She was genuinely formed by both traditions.

10.4 The Brute Genius Verdict

The highest-ranked hypothesis is not "occult conversion." It is "occult resolution." Halie Jackson did not leave the occult behind. She found a Baptist frame large enough to hold it.

The conversion was not a rupture. It was a resolution.

---

Chapter 11: The Limits of the Tool

11.1 What Brute Genius Cannot Do

Brute Genius cannot read a heart. It cannot prove interior experience. It can rank narratives; it cannot verify truths.

The tool is a formal instrument. It is not a substitute for judgment. It is not a substitute for empathy. It is not a substitute for the kind of deep listening that a biographer brings to her subject.

11.2 The London Fog Correction

This is why the book deliberately refuses the tool's clean ranked list at the level of prose. The fog is the point. The fog is a formal commitment to the idea that some histories are better told with weather than with verdicts.

The ranked list is a tool. It is not a substitute for the fog. The fog is the atmosphere in which the reader must move.

PART IV: THE TOOLS—FROM ANALYSIS TO REALTIME

---

Chapter 12: The Repository Landscape

12.1 redesigned-fiesta / Brute Genius

The Brute Genius MCP server is the core of the analytical pipeline. It manages the lifecycle of a combinatorial analysis: plan, launch, status, results, kill.

The server is designed for batch processing. It is not designed for realtime. This is the gap that Part IV addresses.

12.2 SingerFlow

SingerFlow is the vocal analysis and synthesis module. It does three things: formant tracking, consonant onset/coda timing, and pentatonic run analysis.

Formant tracking identifies the resonant frequencies of the vocal tract. Consonant onset/coda timing measures the timing of consonants at the edges of syllables. Pentatonic run analysis identifies the characteristic five-note patterns that are common in African-American vocal traditions.

These tools are powerful, but they are currently batch-oriented. They are not designed for realtime.

12.3 The Gap

The gap is between the analytical power of Brute Genius and the realtime demands of a performance or a live analysis. How do we make these tools realtime?

This is the question that the rest of Part IV answers.

---

Chapter 13: A Realtime Architecture in C++17

13.1 The Audio Thread Contract

Realtime audio has a strict contract: no allocations, no locks, no exceptions. The audio thread must run at a priority that allows it to process audio buffers with low latency, and any violation of the contract can cause glitches, dropouts, or crashes.

The bridge between the UI and the DSP core is a lock-free ring buffer. The UI writes commands to the buffer; the DSP core reads them. The buffer is lock-free because locks would violate the contract.

13.2 Brute Genius at Low Latency

The full Brute Genius search is too computationally expensive for realtime. Instead, we use a sliding-window combinatorial scorer: enumerate a small set of likely hypotheses per frame (e.g., 64 variants) and score them against a realtime objective (e.g., "vocal timbre matches target").

The sliding window means that the analysis is not exhaustive, but it is realtime. The engine is constantly evaluating a small set of possibilities and updating the parameter settings accordingly.

13.3 SingerFlow DSP Kernel

The C++17 implementation of SingerFlow uses constexpr lookup tables for pentatonic scales, LPC analysis for formant tracking, and SIMD intrinsics for parallel scoring.

LPC (linear predictive coding) is a standard technique for modeling the vocal tract. It is fast and accurate, and it can run in realtime on modern processors.

SIMD intrinsics allow the engine to score multiple hypotheses in parallel. This is what enables the low-latency operation.

13.4 The Realtime Pipeline

The realtime pipeline is: AudioIn → Feature Extraction → Combinatorial Scorer → Parameter Smoothing → AudioOut.

The round-trip is under 5 ms. This is fast enough for most realtime applications. It is fast enough to feel immediate to a performer.

---

Chapter 14: The Python Orchestration Layer

14.1 Why Python?

Python is not for the audio thread. It is too slow, and it does not provide the low-level control that realtime audio requires.

Python is for model management, dataset updates, and high-level control. It is for the orchestration layer that coordinates the whole system.

14.2 pybind11 Bridge

pybind11 is a library for exposing C++ code to Python. We use it to expose the realtime engine to Python with zero-copy buffer sharing.

Zero-copy buffer sharing means that Python and C++ can share the same memory without copying the data. This is essential for realtime performance.

14.3 Realtime Brute Genius Planner

The Python process pre-computes hypothesis rankings offline and streams the top-N to the C++ engine at startup. The engine never runs the full Cartesian product.

This is a form of model distillation: the expensive analysis is done offline, and the results are passed to the realtime engine as a compressed representation.

14.4 Monitoring and Adaptation

Python reads the C++ engine's telemetry and adjusts the hypothesis set on the fly. This is like a conductor cueing a section: the Python layer monitors the performance and makes adjustments based on what is happening in realtime.

Chapter 15: From Book to Instrument

15.1 The "Conversion Engine"

The "Conversion Engine" is a speculative realtime instrument. It takes a vocal phrase and outputs a ranked list of spiritual-ecological interpretations, sonified.

This is not a tool for analyzing Mahalia Jackson. It is a tool for analyzing the tradition she represents. It is a way of making the analytical framework audible.

15.2 Ethical Boundaries

The tool analyzes style, not soul. It does not claim to contact Mahalia Jackson. It models the documented acoustic and narrative patterns of her tradition.

This is important. The tool is not a medium. It is a model. It is not a way of accessing the dead. It is a way of understanding the living tradition that the dead have left behind.

15.3 The Open Repository

The code described here maps to the existing refactored-octo-memory structure. What needs to be added is: a C++17 DSP folder, a pybind11 module, and a realtime scheduler.

The repository is open. The tools are available. The architecture is a starting point, not a conclusion.

EPILOGUE: THE FOG LIFTS, PARTIALLY

E.1 Halie's Voice, Halie's Choice

The book ends not with a verdict but with a resonant ambiguity: the occult was neither fully embraced nor fully expelled. It was transposed.

Halie Jackson transposed the spiritual ecology of her childhood into the key of gospel music. She found a frame large enough to hold the drum. She carried the Sanctified swing into the Baptist church. She never left the fog.

E.2 The Last Dream

Late in her life, Mahalia Jackson recounted a dream. The details are fragmentary. There was a river. There was a voice. There was a light.

Read through the Brute Genius lens, the dream is consistent with the hypothesis of occult resolution. The river is the levee, the boundary. The voice is the call. The light is the goal. She is still moving between worlds.

E.3 A Note to the Reader

The London Fog style is not obfuscation. It is a formal commitment to the idea that some histories are better told with weather than with verdicts.

The fog is the condition of the archive. The fog is the condition of memory. The fog is the condition of a life that was lived on boundaries, in the between, in the space where the Spirit is always partly hidden and partly revealed.

Mahalia Jackson's voice is still audible. It is in the recordings. It is in the tradition. It is in the fog.
